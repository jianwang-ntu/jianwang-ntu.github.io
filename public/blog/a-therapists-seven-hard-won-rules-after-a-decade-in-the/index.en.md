# A Therapist's Seven Hard-Won Rules, After a Decade in the Chair

![A Therapist's Seven Hard-Won Rules, After a Decade in the Chair](/images/blog/a-therapists-seven-hard-won-rules-after-a-decade-in-the.png)

An anonymous Chinese-language post making the rounds — attributed only to "a therapist" — lists seven lessons the author says took ten years of clinical work to actually see. The tone is deliberately blunt: the author calls the list "扎心" (literally "stabs the heart"), and frames each item as a truth people resist rather than a technique to master.

The post opens with what the author treats as the foundation of adulthood:

> "Being a mature adult means accepting that you must take full responsibility for everything in your life — even things that aren't your fault."

The remaining six read as corollaries.

**On boundaries.** The author argues that strong, clearly drawn boundaries are what produce high-quality relationships; soft or blurry ones, the post claims, only generate endless drama.

**On unsolvable problems.** Many problems in a life, the author writes, will never be solved. The task is not to keep grinding against them but to learn to keep living while carrying them.

**On the brain's lies.** The post claims the brain lies to its owner daily — insisting the world is ending, that a mistake is fatal, that everyone is laughing. The author's prescription is bracingly short: learn to tell your brain to shut up.

**On persuasion.** Stop trying to convince people to like you, the author writes. The right people don't need convincing; everyone else just finds the effort annoying.

**On killing dreams.** The sixth point is the one the author flags as ugly: sometimes the thing most worth doing is letting a dream die by your own hand. Letting go, the post argues, is the release.

**On who actually matters.** The final item: over a long life, the people who truly matter can be counted on one's fingers. When you find them, the author writes, give them your time, stay close, and be grateful.

## Why this reads the way it does

Nothing in the list is novel on its own — radical responsibility, boundary work, acceptance of the unfixable, and cognitive defusion ("your brain is not your friend") are staples of everything from Stoicism to ACT (Acceptance and Commitment Therapy) to standard CBT. What gives the post its bite is compression: the author collapses a decade of clinical pattern-matching into seven imperatives and refuses to soften any of them. The advice to *let a dream die* is the clearest example — most self-help writing works hard to avoid that sentence.

The author's closing frame is not a call to optimise or achieve. It is a call to narrow: fewer people, firmer lines, less argument with reality, and gratitude for the short list of relationships that survive the filter.
