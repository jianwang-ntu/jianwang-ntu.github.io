# Why Most Profitable Businesses Stay Invisible: Business vs. VC Startup in the AI Era

![Why Most Profitable Businesses Stay Invisible: Business vs. VC Startup in the AI Era](https://publicsg.s3.ap-southeast-1.amazonaws.com/github.io/images/blog/why-most-profitable-businesses-stay-invisible-business-vs.png)

In a recent video titled 《为什么多数赚钱好生意，不会被你看到？》, the host of the channel 课代表立正 argues that AI has changed the economics of starting a company without changing the test every company still has to pass. The talk is less a "how to start a business" walkthrough than an argument about categories: AI makes execution cheaper, but product–market fit still decides what survives.

## AI Lowers the Cost of Building, Not the Bar of the Market

The speaker opens with a shift he sees on both sides of the employment relationship. More of the tasks employees are paid to complete can now be automated, which he argues erodes the defensibility of simply receiving a job and finishing it. At the same time, AI lowers the cost of turning an idea into something operational — work that once needed a sizable team can increasingly be carried by a very small one.

But most people, according to the talk, hold a distorted picture of entrepreneurship. They see two options: the glamorous venture-backed startup, or low-end small businesses and gig work. Between them, the speaker argues, sits a large and largely invisible middle ground of profitable companies that never raise venture money. That invisibility is the question in the video's title.

## "Business" and "VC Startup" Are Different Games

The distinction the speaker draws is not about incorporation, technology, or even whether money has been raised. It is about **growth**.

A profitable business can succeed while staying small. If it serves a limited set of customers and reliably makes money, the speaker treats it as a valid business. A VC-backed startup cannot settle there, because the venture portfolio model depends on a handful of companies producing extreme returns.

| Profitable business | VC startup |
| --- | --- |
| Optimizes for sustainable profit | Optimizes for extraordinary growth |
| Can serve a niche | Usually needs a very large market |
| A ceiling may be acceptable | The ceiling must be extremely high |
| Cash flow matters | Growth, valuation and exit matter |
| Founder retains more control | Capital imposes growth expectations |
| Success can mean a very good living | Success usually means acquisition, IPO or huge enterprise value |

This framing explains something that otherwise looks paradoxical: a technically excellent, already-profitable company can still be a poor venture investment. The two models are being scored against different scoreboards.

## PMF Is the Shared Foundation

The recurring equation in the talk is that business and startup differ in their growth requirements, but both rest on product–market fit.

The speaker is careful to separate PMF from encouragement. People saying an idea sounds good is not evidence. He recounts a sales experience of his own to make the point behavioral: pitching broad consulting services drew lukewarm reactions and slow follow-ups, but when enterprise AI training came up, customer behavior changed immediately — questions started before the presentation had finished, and the conversation moved to when work could start and how it would be implemented.

That shift is the signal he wants founders to watch for:

- **Weak PMF:** the founder chases customers.
- **Strong PMF:** customers start pulling the solution out of the founder.

The talk offers a sharper test still: if the product is imperfect and inconvenient, do customers fight through the friction anyway? If they do, the speaker suggests, there is probably a serious underlying need.

## Technical Difficulty Is Not Market Value

This may be the section most directly aimed at technical founders. The speaker warns that highly educated and technical people tend to equate solving a hard problem with creating value, and he rejects that equation outright. Difficulty measures difficulty. Value is determined by whether somebody needs the result — it is defined by the market, not by the engineering.

Something can be scientifically sophisticated, technically novel and extremely hard to build, and still have almost no commercial value. Something technically trivial can be enormously valuable if it resolves an urgent problem.

The reordering he proposes is simple: the first question is not how impressive the technology is, but who urgently wants the outcome.

## PMF Eventually Has to Show Up in the Numbers

The talk then moves from qualitative signals to measurable behavior. For startups, the speaker argues PMF surfaces most clearly through retention — customers who genuinely need a product keep using or buying it.

He offers a simple equilibrium argument: a product's user ceiling is roughly monthly new users divided by monthly churn rate. Acquiring 10,000 users a month while losing 10% of the base monthly tends toward an equilibrium near 100,000 users. Spending more on acquisition without fixing retention, on this logic, mostly buys faster churn.

The principle he draws from it is blunt: growth cannot permanently repair weak PMF. Marketing can push the curve up temporarily, but it cannot make people stay with a product they do not want.

## For a Profitable Business, PMF Looks Like Repurchase and Trust

The equivalent signal outside the venture path, the speaker says, is repeat purchase rate combined with lifetime value. That leads him to a conclusion he states plainly: the long-term core of a business is trust.

His reasoning is that a business usually has a naturally bounded customer population. Improving lifetime economics therefore means those same customers must trust it enough to come back, renew, or buy the next thing. He argues against extracting maximum short-term revenue for that reason — squeezing a customer may improve one sales period while destroying the repeat purchases that would have followed.

The compounding chain he describes runs: value → trust → retention and repurchase → lifetime value → sustainable profit.

## The VC Scoreboard: Growth, ARR Quality and Exit

On the venture path, growth becomes an explicit quantitative constraint. The speaker cites the classic SaaS "3-3-2-2-2" illustration — roughly 300% growth in each of the first two years, then 200% in the following three.

The exact benchmark matters less than the lesson: taking venture money changes the definition of good performance. Investors track a chain that runs from market size to ARR, ARR quality, growth rate, retention, the next financing round, enterprise value and finally exit.

He adds a warning specific to the current moment: ARR figures at AI companies can mislead, because a company may annualize a month of immature or promotional revenue. Revenue quantity alone is not enough — revenue quality is what is being judged.

## Choosing a Model Is Choosing a Life

The least financial and possibly deepest argument in the talk is that this choice is a lifestyle decision. Once venture capital is raised, the speaker describes an externally reinforced sequence: a B round sets a growth target, which sets up a C round and a larger target, then the next round, then an exit. The founder can effectively become an employee of the company's growth expectations — pushed, as he puts it, to keep running, because the pressure never stops.

A bootstrapped, profitable business may have a much lower ceiling but considerably more control over pace, team size and personal time. AI strengthens that alternative: work that once required HR, accounting, operations, developers, designers and administrators can increasingly be compressed into a far smaller organization, which is why the speaker sees the one-person company — the 超级个体 — as newly viable.

## Five Questions the Speaker Suggests Asking First

The video closes on a checklist to run before committing to a direction:

1. Who urgently wants this outcome?
2. Will customers keep using it or buy again?
3. Is this a good business, or something that can become large?
4. Is venture capital actually needed, or can this fund itself?
5. What kind of work and life is the founder choosing?

The ordering implied by the talk is PMF, then economics, then growth ceiling, then capital model, then lifestyle — rather than technology, then product, then fundraising, then hoping somebody wants it.

## Why This Matters

The through-line is that AI makes execution cheaper, and cheap execution is a weak moat. If nearly everyone can build faster, the scarce advantages shift toward PMF, distribution, trust, proprietary relationships and data, retention, and business-model design.

The question a founder faces changes accordingly: not "can this be built," but "should this exist, who desperately needs it, and can that value be captured repeatedly?"

## Conclusion

The speaker's argument is that AI has moved the cost of starting a company without moving the standard for succeeding at one. Business and startup remain different games with different growth requirements and different definitions of winning, but both are settled by whether customers actually want the thing and come back for it. AI makes the one-person company and the 超级个体 more realistic than before — and, on the talk's account, leaves PMF, trust and retention exactly where they were: at the root.
